# 2. The visitor

I took the storm lantern from its hook and told myself I was only going to the door. It was the first sentence I had spoken aloud in eleven days, and it came out cracked, like something left too long in the cold.

Bram was already there. He stood stiff-legged with his good ear turned to the wood and his grey hackles lifting along his spine, and he did not bark. He is an old dog and he saves it.

"Stay," I said. He didn't, quite. He pressed against my knee instead.

The wind took the door out of my hand the moment I drew the bolt. Rain came in sideways, hard enough to sting, and the whole rock seemed to lean away from the northwest. I wedged my shoulder into the gap and held the lantern high. The great lamp above me was dark, and the dark it left behind was total.

The small light was at the foot of the steps by then.

I had watched it from the window, a steady yellow point that never swung, never stumbled, never hurried. The causeway had been under the sea for a good while. I knew that water. A person does not walk it. A person does not even wade it.

Yet the light climbed, and behind it came a shape: a dark coat, a hood pulled low, a figure leaning into the weather like someone who had done it before. I couldn't see a face. I could see that the coat was streaming, and that the hand holding the light was bare.

"The causeway's shut," I said. My voice was steadier the second time. "It's been shut since twenty to midnight."

The figure stopped two steps below me. The light dipped, politely, so it wasn't in my eyes.

"I know," they said.

Two words, flattened by the wind, in a voice I could place nowhere: not young, not old. Bram made a low sound in his chest that I had never heard from him. Then he wagged, once, slowly, and stopped, as if he had remembered something.

"You'll want to come in," I heard myself say, because that is what you say on a rock, because the sea does not care who you are.

"If it's no trouble."

I stepped back. The visitor came up the last two steps and over the threshold, and the lantern light fell across a wet sleeve, a wet collar, and a hem that, when I looked down, was not dripping at all.

I decided I had not seen that. I bolted the door against the gale.
