# 2. The visitor

I did not go to the door straight away. I stood at the window with the poker still in my hand and watched the light come closer.

It kept a pace I couldn't fault, neither hurried nor careful, and it did not swing. Anyone walking that causeway, even at low water in daylight, holds a torch the way you hold a cup on a moving boat, with the beam jumping at every step. This one stayed level. The rain went past it sideways and it did not so much as flicker. Where the causeway meets the rock it paused, then began to climb the steps cut into the landing, and I lost it below the sill.

Bram had got up. He stood by the stove with his head turned, not toward the window now but toward the door, and his tail was quite still.

I put the poker back on its hook. I lit the storm lantern on the table and carried it down the passage to the door, because I could not think what else to do. The knock came when I was three steps from it. Three knocks, evenly spaced, not loud.

When I drew the bolt the wind took the door out of my hand and threw it back against the wall. A woman stood on the step. She was holding a small lamp of a kind I didn't recognise, brass, with a glass chimney, and its flame stood straight up in the gale. Her coat was dark with rain across the shoulders. Below the knee it was dry.

"I'm sorry to come so late," she said. "I saw you had no light."

It was the first voice I had heard in eleven days, and I found I had nothing ready to say back to it. I asked her whether she was hurt. She said she wasn't. I asked her how she had come across, and she looked past me, down the passage, as if she had not heard the question, and said, "Is that your dog?"

Bram had followed me. He was standing at my heel, and he had turned his head so that his left ear was toward her, and he was listening.

I stepped back and let her in. I don't know, even now, that I decided to. I pushed the door shut against the wind and slid the bolt, and by the time I came back along the passage she was in the kitchen, standing by the stove as if she knew where the warmth would be. She had set her lamp on the table. I put mine down beside it, and hers was the brighter of the two.
