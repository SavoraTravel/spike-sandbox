# 2. The visitor

I took the storm lantern from its hook and went down, because there was nothing else to do with my hands.

Bram came as far as the bottom stair and stopped, the way he does when he has decided a thing is my business. I left him there with his head cocked toward the door, his good ear turned to whatever the wind was carrying.

The gale hit me the moment I drew the bolt. Rain came across the rock in sheets, and the lantern guttered and steadied and guttered again. Out on the causeway the small light had not changed its pace. It was steady, unhurried, a little too level for someone walking over weed and stone in the dark. I told myself it was the way the rain bent it.

I counted to twelve, out of habit, and the light was at the foot of the rock.

I had meant to call out. Eleven days of silence had left my voice somewhere I couldn't easily reach, and when it came it was a croak. "Who's there?"

The light stopped. Then it lowered, and I saw a figure behind it, wrapped to the chin in something dark and heavy with water. A hood, a hand, the lamp. I could not have said whether it was a man or a woman, young or old. The face stayed in the shadow of the hood, and I did not like to push the lantern toward it.

"I saw your window," the visitor said. The voice was quiet, and it carried against the wind in a way that voices shouldn't. "Yours was the only light."

That was not true, and we both knew it, because mine was the only light on Skerra Rock and the one that mattered had been dark for three nights. But I stood aside from the door.

I should have asked about the tide. I should have asked where they had come from, and what boat, and what road, and why the water had not stopped them. The questions sat in my mouth like stones. What I said instead, God help me, was, "There's tea."

The visitor stepped over the threshold. The water running off that cloak made no sound on the flagstones, or else the gale swallowed it.

Behind me, Bram had not moved from the stair. He was not growling. He was only watching, with his head low and his whole grey body very still, and I understood that for once he did not need his ears.

I shut the door on the weather. The bolt went home. It was twenty past midnight.
